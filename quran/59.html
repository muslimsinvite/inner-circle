Movement I · Verses 59:1–5

External Fortresses Collapse When Hearts Lose Anchoring

Surah Al-Hashr opens in the past tense of cosmic praise: everything in the heavens and earth has already exalted Allah, the Exalted in Might, the Wise (59:1). Immediately, the lens narrows to a concrete historical rupture in al-Madinah: the expulsion (al-jalā’) of a disbelieving faction among the People of the Scripture from their homes at “the first gathering” (li-awwali al-ḥashr, 59:2).

Notice the psychological irony preserved in verse 59:2. Both sides miscalculated based on physical appearances. The believers did not imagine the entrenched residents would leave, and the residents believed their fortified walls (حُصُونُهُمْ) made them invulnerable. Yet Divine decree arrived “from where they had not expected”—not by breaching stone walls from outside, but by casting terror (al-ru‘b) inside their hearts. In dismantling their own homes before departing, they enacted externally the spiritual collapse already underway within.

Verse 59:5 addresses an operational question of that siege: whether the believers cut down palm trees (līnah) or left them standing on their roots, both occurred by Divine permission—teaching moral accountability even in tactical decisions.

Expectation · Verse 59:2

“You did not think they would leave”

Measuring purely by material strength—walled settlements, stockpiles, and long-standing roots—the believers assumed an evacuation without prolonged warfare was impossible. The verse teaches people of insight (ūlī al-abṣār) not to mistake physical entrenchment for permanence.

“It is He who expelled the ones who disbelieved among the People of the Scripture from their homes at the first gathering. You did not think they would leave, and they thought that their fortresses would protect them from Allah; but [the decree of] Allah came upon them from where they had not expected, and He cast terror into their hearts [so] they destroyed their houses by their [own] hands and the hands of the believers. So take warning, O people of vision.”

Movement II · Verses 59:6–10

Socioeconomic Justice: Circulating Fay’ Beyond the Wealthy

When property is vacated without combat—without spurring horses or camels—the Quran classifies it as Fay’ (59:6). Because no individual soldier won it on the battlefield, no private claim of conquest applies. Instead, verse 59:7 establishes one of the foundational economic mandates in the Quran: wealth must be channeled toward public and vulnerable beneficiaries “so that it will not be a perpetual distribution among the rich from among you” (كَيْ لَا يَكُونَ دُولَةً بَيْنَ الْأَغْنِيَاءِ مِنْكُمْ).

Verses 59:7–8 explicitly identify the recipients: Allah and His Messenger (public governance), near relatives, orphans, the needy (al-masākīn), the stranded traveler, and specifically the poor emigrants (al-fuqarā’ al-muhājirīn) who had been expelled from their own homes and properties. Economic restitution directly heals forced displacement.

Crucially, economic policy is sustained by inner virtue. Verse 59:9 honors the settled hosts of al-Madinah (al-Anṣār) for īthār—giving preference to newcomers even while experiencing privation themselves—and identifies the root of social prosperity as protection from “the stinginess of one’s soul” (shuḥḥ nafsihī). Verse 59:10 extends this bond forward to every future generation who prays for unity without resentment (ghill).

The Three-Tiered Moral Community · 59:8–10
Verse 59:8

The Poor Emigrants (Al-Muhājirūn)

Expelled from homes and wealth, yet motivated by seeking Allah’s bounty and approval rather than bitterness. Their integrity earns the Quranic title: The Truthful (Al-Ṣādiqūn).

Verse 59:9

The Settled Hosts (Al-Anṣār)

Anchored in al-Madinah and faith beforehand. They love the newcomers, harbor zero envy over Fay’ allocations, and practice īthār despite privation. Freed from soul-stinginess (shuḥḥ), they are The Successful (Al-Mufliḥūn).

Verse 59:10

Subsequent Generations

Believers across time remain spiritually bound to the founding community through a specific prayer: seeking forgiveness for predecessors and cleansing hearts of any resentment (ghill) toward fellow believers.

Movement III · Verses 59:11–17

Moral Psychology: Hypocrisy, Fear Inversion & Shattered Hearts

Having portrayed a community unified by selflessness, Surah Al-Hashr pivots sharply to dissect its opposite: the psychology of hypocrisy (nifāq). Verses 59:11–17 expose how outward bravado conceals profound inner cowardice, fragmented loyalties, and self-deception.

Diagnostic Portrait · Verses 59:13–14

Three Symptoms of Unanchored Hearts

1 · Fear Inversion (59:13)

“You [believers] are more fearful within their breasts than Allah.” Replacing reverence for the Creator with dread of human opinion stems from failing to understand (lā yafqahūn).

2 · Walled Defensive Posturing (59:14)

They avoid open conviction, engaging only “within fortified cities or from behind walls” (min warā’i judur)—hiding moral fragility behind physical or social barriers.

3 · Illusory Unity (59:14)

“You think they are together, but their hearts are diverse” (تَحْسَبُهُمْ جَمِيعًا وَقُلُوبُهُمْ شَتَّى). Alliances built on expediency—not shared truth—breed severe internal hostility (ba’suhum baynahum shadīd).

Movement IV · Verses 59:18–21

Moral Awakening: Self-Forgetfulness & The Mountain Parable

After diagnosing hypocrisy, the surah turns directly to the believer’s conscience with a double call to taqwā (God-consciousness): “Let every soul look to what it has put forth for tomorrow” (li-ghad, 59:18). Calling the Hereafter “tomorrow” compresses cosmic eternity into the urgency of the very next morning.

Verse 59:19 then delivers one of the most penetrating psychological laws in scripture: “And be not like those who forgot Allah, so He made them forget themselves.” (نَسُوا اللَّهَ فَأَنْسَاهُمْ أَنْفُسَهُمْ). Forgetting the Creator is never merely a theological lapse—it fractures self-knowledge, causing a person to neglect what truly saves and fulfills their own soul.

Verse 59:21 crystallizes this awakening in the iconic Mountain Parable. If the Quran had been sent down upon a towering mountain—hardest of earthly forms—you would have seen it khāshi‘an (humbled in reverence) and mutaṣaddi‘an (splitting asunder) from awe of Allah. Why, then, can a human heart hear revelation and remain unmoved?

The Mountain Parable · 59:21

لَوْ أَنْزَلْنَا هَذَا الْقُرْآنَ عَلَىٰ جَبَلٍ لَرَأَيْتَهُ خَاشِعًا مُتَصَدِّعًا مِنْ خَشْيَةِ اللَّهِ وَتِلْكَ الْأَمْثَالُ نَضْرِبُهَا لِلنَّاسِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ

“If We had sent down this Qur’an upon a mountain, you would have seen it humbled and coming apart from fear of Allah. And these examples We present to the people that perhaps they will give thought.”

Movement V · Verses 59:22–24

The Concluding Tapestry: Fifteen Divine Names & Attributes

Having called humanity to give thought (yatafakkarūn, 59:21), Surah Al-Hashr answers the deepest question of the heart: Who is the Lord before Whom mountains tremble? Verses 59:22–24 present one of the most concentrated litanies of Al-Asmā’ al-Ḥusnā (the Most Beautiful Names) in the entire Quran—progressing from intimate knowledge and mercy, to absolute sovereignty and peace, to creative design, before sealing the ring composition with cosmic glorification (59:24).
