Section II · Structural Parallels

How Dhī al-Ma‘ārij answers human restlessness with nine ascending disciplines

Surah Al-Ma‘ārij mirrors cosmic verticality inside the human soul. Left unchecked, human nature oscillates violently between panic and hoarding (halū‘an). Only the nine virtues anchored by prayer stabilize that oscillation and restore upward ascent.

Lexical Thread: S-’-L

From Mockery to Mercy

v. 1 opens with a skeptic asking (sa’ala sā’ilun) in defiance; v. 10 depicts the Day no friend asks (lā yas’alu) of a friend; v. 25 redeems the root in giving to the petitioner (lis-sā’ili).

Cosmic ↔ Moral Axis

Ascending vs. Turning Back

Angels and the Spirit ascend (ta‘ruju, v. 4) to Allah, whereas the condemned turned his back and retreated (adbara wa tawallā, v. 17) into hoarding (v. 18).

Horizon Symmetry

Time & Orbital Mastery

Opening with the 50,000-year Day of ascent (v. 4) and nearness (qarīban, v. 7), closing with the Lord of all risings and settings (al-mashāriq wal-maghārib, v. 40).

The Unanchored Soul · Vv. 19–21

Halū‘an: The Dual Extremes

Verse 19 introduces a rare Quranic intensive adjective—halū‘an (restless, anxious, unsteady)—and verses 20–21 immediately define it through two symmetrical failures:

جَزُوعًا
Jazū‘an (v. 20)

When harm or hardship touches him: impatient, panicking, losing inward composure.

مَنُوعًا
Manū‘an (v. 21)

When good or abundance touches him: tight-fisted, hoarding (linking back to jama‘a fa-aw‘ā in v. 18).

The Structural Remedy (Vv. 22–35)

Notice the exception particle Illā al-muṣallīn (“Except the observers of prayer,” v. 22). Prayer stands at the entry (v. 23, inward constancy dā’imūn) and the exit (v. 34, outward vigilance yuḥāfiẓūn), enclosing seven ethical and eschatological virtues that neutralize both panic and greed.

Section IV · Exegetical Synthesis

Four classical tafsīr keys unlocking Surah Al-Ma‘ārij

Synthesizing linguistic observations and classical commentary (including Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī, and al-Rāzī) around the surah’s core theological tensions.

1 · Vv. 1–5

Occasion of Challenge & Ṣabran Jamīlan

Classical commentators link verse 1 to Meccan opponents (notably al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith) who sarcastically challenged revelation by demanding immediate divine punishment. Instead of answering mockery with haste, Allah answers with His transcendent title Dhī al-Ma‘ārij—revealing that cosmic governance unfolds across measures like fifty thousand years, and instructing the Prophet to adopt ṣabran jamīlan: patience free of complaint to creation, anchored in certainty.

2 · Vv. 6–18

Perceptual Inversion: Distant vs. Near

“They see it as distant (ba‘īdan), but We see it as near (qarīban).” To human myopia, accountability feels remote; to divine omniscience, every assured reality is already imminent. When that Day arrives, sky and mountains lose density (molten dregs and carded wool), social shelters dissolve so violently that a criminal would trade child, spouse, brother, clan, and all humanity—yet kallā: the blazing core fire (Laẓā) specifically calls those who turned away and hoarded.

3 · Vv. 19–21

The Quran’s Self-Gloss of Halū‘an

Philologists note that halū‘ appears only once in the Quran, on the intensive pattern fa‘ūl indicating ingrained propensity. Rather than leaving its nuance ambiguous, the text immediately defines it in the next two verses: panic when touched by harm (jazū‘an) and withholding when touched by good (manū‘an). Both reactions share one root defect—measuring reality solely by immediate comfort rather than divine trust.

4 · Vv. 22–44

The Ring of Prayer & Sovereign Replacement

Prayer both opens (dā’imūn, inward constancy, v. 23) and closes (yuḥāfiẓūn, diligent guardianship of times and integrity, v. 34) the nine virtues. Between those pillars stand financial justice, eschatological realism, moral modesty, covenantal fidelity, and truthful testimony. In the closing scene, disbelievers who rushed into mocking circles (‘izīn, v. 37) are reminded of their humble origin (v. 39) and warned by the Lord of all sunrises and sunsets (v. 40) that He can replace them with better people—before emerging from graves as swiftly as they once ran to idols.
